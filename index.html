I scaled revenue from 5M to 22M in 1 year.

Here's what I solved to make that happen.

One thing for you to have in mind as you read this, the organizations I worked in, at the time, had a ZERO AI policy, so everything you'll read below was done at a time where people still got into the weeds and solved things themselves, and not just push out a prompt (something I am now very proficient in and do a fair amount of advising on) - realistically, the reason I'm good at prompt engineering and AI Enablement now, is because all of it is informed from years of being in the middle of everything, and knowing exactly what has to happen to get a good outcome.

MoM MTD Company Wide Standings

YTD Rank: One of the best in the entire country

I inherited a team of 20 sales reps when stepping into this role and their MoM performance for the last two quarters was wildly inconsistent. They had hit 5M the year prior, facilitated by a new pricing structure that was launched which made the process simplified, which allowed even this inconsistent team to organically hit 5M.

The last person to lead them was pretty content with that as it meant they hit their quotas and had no heat on them, even though everything about it, when looked closely, signaled to a broken down coaching cadence and a reliance on Aggressive marketing initiatives and pricing structures at a time that Charter was trying to capture back lost business and a 56% dip in stock value to deliver results.

Not being one to rely on market conditions or attrition for meeting performance, I immediately began creating standards for how they should handle customers, what the customer journey should look like, a more natural discovery rhythm and most importantly a recurring weekly meeting where we'd all, as a group, go over the most voted on objections they encountered or difficult situations they wanted guidance on. (I built a website with a form and voting function to allow them to vote on this and had quick links to a custom performance tracker I made them, too - one which auto reported outcomes in real time, and updated in real time, too -- but I'll talk more about that later).

After some months of targeted growth, I was able to turn this team into one that was a lot more independent, and with better judgement. My goal was never to make a team that had all the answers, though - it was to make one that knew how to respond to feeling immense pressure and to get out of uncertainty in a way that doesn't jeopardize the customer journey.

Graciously, after some months, my day to day life went from answering roughly 50 questions an hour to only being brought in for intervention and skill gaps shortening to where I was only queried just over 10 times an hour.

What This Taught Me

I learned that inconsistent performance is rarely a talent problem—it's almost always a systems problem.

People naturally optimize for whatever environment they're placed in. If expectations are unclear, coaching is inconsistent, or success isn't repeatable, performance will fluctuate regardless of how talented the team is.

Since then, whenever I enter a new organization, I don't start by asking who my best and worst performers are. I start by asking whether the system itself consistently produces good decisions. If it doesn't, I focus on building standards, coaching rhythms, and feedback loops before trying to coach individuals. The better the operating system, the less dependent the business becomes on exceptional people.

At Charter, and in Insurance, we'd only get paid if the business stayed sold. Within insurance, we'd actually get hit with Chargebacks if they canceled within the time it was written. Honestly, I'm grateful to have spent so many years in Insurance, because it really established what Lifetime Value meant, and how the customer journey needs to be structured and handled in such a way that keeps them feeling confident in their choice to do business with us and renew for many years to come.

I created a training to help them navigate how to handle customers, informed not only by the almost 1400 policies that I wrote personally, but from the Millions of dollars of AP I had to retain annually because it was at risk of leaving for things that had nothing to do with me, and almost always triggered by either the acquisition strategy, or the sales process itself and how it was handled by the rep who initiated the policy. When you are dealing with irate customers who want nothing to do with you as a business, a lot of truth comes to light, and I'm glad for the years of exposure I've had to the dirty dark parts of sales that let me know what never to do when writing my own business.

Ultimately, the activation and retention rate within 3 months was around 72%, after me reinforcing how to speak with customers with the end goal in mind, with expectations established early on, and checking for understanding and changing from pitching to educating a customer on how to make an informed decision (one that conveniently ends with us being the solution) my Activation and 3 month Retention rating went all the way up to 93%.

What This Taught Me

Retention is won long before a customer decides whether to cancel.

Customers rarely leave because of a single interaction. More often, they leave because expectations were never established, uncertainty wasn't addressed, or they never fully understood the decision they were making.

That fundamentally changed how I think about sales. I stopped viewing sales as the art of persuasion and started viewing it as the responsibility of guiding customers toward informed decisions. Today, whenever I design training or enablement, I work backwards from the customer experience I want six months after the sale, then build the sales process that creates it.

Just in case you've never worked at a Fortune 50 company that dominates the market with tens of millions of customers, here's the kind of activity that happened on average for JUST our department.

88,000 Calls Daily
11,000 Per Site
2,000 Per Branch
400 Per Frontline Leader
9.7 Hours Talk Time Daily

We operated on a 4x10 schedule.

So there was virtually no down time in between calls, and there's fires to put out virtually every handful of minutes. Not to mention, severe amounts of executive pressure from Site VPs and Directors - the kind that isn't always communicated well, with the hint of what may happen if you don't improve.

Ultimately, we're responsible for ensuring that our performance is always above water, and that we're never the outliers across the site, and then across the organization, because there are hundreds of fires happening in real time at any given moment and no one wants to be the outlier. Pretty stressful, eh?

Funnily enough, that's not even the problem. I don't mind stress, I actually have what my doctor described as the lowest possible threshold for cortisol a human can have, with actual concerns that I may have an adrenal problem. I literally handle stress very well - and I know this scientifically -- anyway, the challenge is:

How do I find time to have these huddles, communicate direction, problem solve, handle customers, do overrides, handle our backend admin program (CSG) when there's order entry errors, and also handling interaction audits, and corporate initiatives and intradepartmental audits all while ensuring your team is performing consistently?

Well, the short answer is, it requires more than 10 hours of work we do -- I honestly ended up working round 14 hours daily just to stay on top of it, something that many others didn't and found themselves severely behind and constantly missing deliverables.

So, how do you make time? Simple, I find ways to be more proficient at the technology we have and the restrictions we have in place for doing so by mastering Excel and creating dashboards and knowledge bases that are easier to search than company made ones, and streamlining their decision making ability so that I don't become the bottleneck.

I designed a tracker that they could place all their activity into, all they had to do was put the outcome of the call. The Excel Sheet automatically populated the rest and an update and also fed into our other reporting systems so that I didn't have to do any manual reporting on anything, more importantly than that, I coded into it goals which were real and based on where they were MTD, which always put daily goals to be on pace for hitting quota based on where they personally were, and also giving visibility how above they were. I even had personalized messages that would display depending on the kind of performance they had (i.e. if they were selling a lot of PSUs but had very low Mobile Attachment, a message regarding how to pitch it and a popup displaying that they didn't get one if they kept not selling it on subsequent entries to reinforce the need to maintain a 100% Pitch Rate).

This reduced some of the admin work I had to deal with and allowed me to focus entirely on side by sides, live listening and auditing their progress towards individual game plans I had made for each of them based on where their weaknesses were.

On top of that, for the reps who understood WHY the answer was the answer it was, and not simply wanting the handout of what to say and were able to intuit the reasoning and apply it to the next applicable scenario, I created a reward system where the best 3 reps of the week could get off-phone activity which would be to be assigned to 2-3 other reps and handle their side by sides and help mentor them through the process. When you have to talk for almost 10 hours a day, any time off the phones for reps is a huge blessing, especially if it's done to help them learn new skills and responsibilities and gain some insight into the nightmare that it is to do the job so they're better at how they approach things to me because they've had first hand experience of what it's like in my shoes on a small scale (this is also a very handy tip to get multiple departments to collaborate together and work on good handoff procedures).

That compounding with dedicated coaching sessions and team wide trainings to improve their judgement, I ended up finding myself with a lot more time to fully dedicate to their development and get the results I was able to achieve with them. Something that was impossible unless I solved all of these other things first.

What This Taught Me

Time management was never the real problem.

Every leader eventually reaches a point where there is simply more work than hours available. Working longer can solve that temporarily, but it never scales.

The real solution is eliminating low-value work through better systems.

Since then, I instinctively look for anything repetitive, manual, or dependent on one person's memory. If a process can be automated, standardized, or simplified, it should be. Every hour saved through operational improvement becomes another hour invested in coaching, strategic thinking, and developing people—the areas where leaders create the greatest long-term value.

To echo off a prior sentiment, we don't get paid unless business sticks for an extended period of time. When it comes to what pays us the most, Mobile, the team had a disgustingly low figure. This ended up being acceptable by everyone because mobile had a unique position, it was the clearest revenue opportunity within the organization and was also one of the most operationally constrained.

Mobile represented one of our highest-value products, but despite strong customer interest and successful sales conversations, activation completion rates were significantly underperforming.

Customers were saying yes. The sales team was creating demand.. but somewhere between the initial purchase decision and completed activation, the customer journey was breaking down. At the time, wireless activation adoption was approximately 37%, creating a significant gap between sold business and realized recurring revenue.

The challenge was that this was not a traditional sales performance issue. The barriers impacting activation existed across multiple parts of the customer journey — including activation processes, technical dependencies, operational workflows, compliance requirements, and customer understanding.

The question became:

How do we improve an outcome owned by the entire organization while ensuring frontline teams remain accountable for the parts they can influence?

The first step was understanding where customers were falling out of the journey. Rather than assuming the issue was a sales execution problem, I worked to understand the complete lifecycle:

Where were customers experiencing friction?
At what point were activations failing?
Which issues were within frontline control?
Which issues required cross-functional intervention?

Through customer journey analysis, call review, and collaboration with key stakeholders, we identified recurring breakdowns that were preventing successful activation completion.

The issue was not one department failing. It was a lack of shared ownership across the entire customer experience.

I organized and led a cross-functional task force across:

Sales
Retention
Activation
Operations
Compliance
Technical Support
FCC stakeholders

The goal was not simply to identify problems. The goal was to create ownership around solving them.

Together, we:

Reviewed customer interactions to identify recurring failure points
Analyzed activation journeys from purchase through completion
Established accountability around unresolved issues
Improved communication between teams responsible for different stages of the customer experience

While cross-functional alignment was critical, the frontline team still needed clarity around their role. A common challenge in operational environments is that when employees encounter obstacles outside of their control, frustration can replace ownership.

My focus was ensuring the team understood:

"We cannot control every part of the process, but we can control how we guide customers through it."

We reinforced:

Setting clearer expectations during the sales process
Educating customers rather than simply completing transactions
Recognizing where customer uncertainty originated
Escalating issues with better context and ownership

The goal was not to make employees responsible for every failure.
The goal was to make sure every employee understood their influence over the customer experience.

After months of iterations, biweekly meetings and analyzing the data at each stage to inform if our method was working, Mobile activation ended up going from 37% all the way to 85%.

The total impact of that was an increased revenue production sitewide of over $10M

What This Taught Me

Some of the biggest revenue opportunities don't require selling more—they require realizing more of what has already been sold.

This experience reinforced that organizational problems rarely respect departmental boundaries. Customers experience one journey, even when businesses are divided into dozens of functions.

Since then, I've become intentional about understanding the entire customer lifecycle before optimizing any individual part of it. Revenue grows fastest when teams stop optimizing for their own metrics and start taking collective ownership of customer outcomes.

Anyone who gets paid on revenue production can feel the pain of a team that takes too long to be good at their job and deliver results.

Thankfully, after some time building success, I was able to turn my enablement strategy into a process that was easy to facilitate.

The outcomes I had prove my method, but let me tell you about the format I chose to use.

At Allstate I had made a training platform for new agents who were struggling, too -- and the LMS format I used resonated very well with agents. Now in our hectic work environment, I had to make it bite sized and referenceable to serve as a "Field Guide" for quick reference, and so I began building the same system for my agents and it greatly improved their ability to get a good read of what kind of buyer they had, what ICP profile they fell into, and how to navigate the conversation based on what's important to them and guide them through a solution.

Since my departure from Charter, I've since advised multiple companies across different industries on this process and built enablement systems for them, too. I have deployed over 20 LMSs this year across multiple industries: Telecom Construction, Telecom Sales, Foundation Repair, Window and Door Replacement, Mobile/Video sales, Trailer Sales, Insurance, Cabinets, Real Estate, Medical Device Sales, FinTech SaaS, and many more.

You can explore more about some of the programs I've published on my website's home page.

What This Taught Me

Training should reduce dependency, not create it.

Too often, onboarding teaches people what to say without helping them understand why it works. That creates employees who perform well in familiar situations but struggle the moment something changes.

That experience shaped how I approach enablement today. My goal is never to build people who memorize scripts—it is to develop judgment. When people understand the principles behind good decisions, they become adaptable, require less supervision, and continue improving long after formal training has ended.

For anyone who's had to sit in a room with Executive leadership after missing forecast (again), you'll know it's a very uncomfortable situation, and it's an easy way for you to lose credibility.

Every month I heard "I should finish around 140", then they'd finish at 80k. The problem was rarely dishonesty, the problem has multiple layers actually. Either the rep knows the real number and they say a number at or exceeding goal to have an easier conversation and not face scrutiny or backlash, compounding all the way up, due to a culture that rewards the signal, and not the outcome. Or.. the rep doesn't know the real number because they can't read between the lines and see actual opportunity health.

It's usually a combination of the both, fixing a culture is difficult, thankfully the latter has a clear path to being solved.

I had to get pretty strategic and think inwardly to what worked when I was selling and when I was in their shoes. I inherently understood the problem they were facing, and what the consequences of inaction were. More importantly, I had a pretty good understanding on what business was like for them and what their day to day life was, and I used that information against them, and to have a lot more valid qualifying steps for advancing a deal forward.

Rather than assuming my reps were exactly like me, I began pressure testing for it. I began asking a lot more questions to check if they understood the prospects business, and what the impact of inaction is, not just that, but what is your "buy in" -- have each of your outreaches and contacts and demos been frontloaded with all the boring hoopla of this is who we are, this is what we do, this is who we've helped, here's our product blablablablablabla -- the prospect doesn't care. Talk to them about something they experience in their day to day life at work that we're positioned to solve. Lead with the pain, teach them, and guide them through the process.

If the reps couldn't answer what the champion stands to gain from implementing our solution, why they are looking to do it now, what they've tried before - if never before - what will be the deciding factor of who they go with, what commitments have actually been made, and what things could happen which could entirely make the deal fall apart - then... I wouldn't forecast it, and let them know they had more discovery and more clear mutual agreements before moving this along further.

Also, I began grading them based on Projected vs Actual, and if they consistently missed their own forecast, they'd get accountability steps, too -- so now they had some skin in the game to be on board with doing this right.

What This Taught Me

Forecasting isn't a revenue exercise—it's a decision quality exercise.

The more organizations I worked with, the more I realized that inaccurate forecasts were almost never caused by bad math. They were caused by weak discovery, assumptions being mistaken for commitments, and managers rewarding confidence instead of evidence. Forecasts became emotional rather than operational.

Since then, I've stopped treating forecasting as a reporting exercise and started treating it as a coaching opportunity. Every forecast should reveal where a salesperson's thinking breaks down, what assumptions still exist, and what information is missing before a customer is truly qualified. When forecasting becomes a byproduct of sound discovery instead of optimism, leadership gains something far more valuable than predictability—they gain trust in the data they're using to make business decisions.

As business owners, and honestly with how AI has evolved, just being anyone associated with a company that is marketable to and showcasing that affiliation online - you know how this next one feels like.

You get a call or DM (I'd say Email, but you and I both know we just put all of those in spam or delete before ever giving them any attention at all) and you know what, it's a product you've heard about, it is regarding something that needs fixing - but the rep didn't lead with that, just word vomit pitch, but you let it move forward to a demo because the problem does need fixing. Then... that demo comes around and it feels like an interrogation.

Don't get me wrong, I feel discovery is the most valuable skill any rep can have. I just don't think in B2B it's the appropriate first hand to show. I should have some idea on how these things play out already, and I should have had enough experience with this that when I talk about it, it's like any average Tuesday - they won't even have to ask me the ever-common "we'd love to see how you've solved this for other companies facing our exact problem first" because... why would they? The confidence in my delivery and the understanding I showed for what falls apart for them if this isn't fixed and what a day in their life looks like let them know I can handle this.

Cool, you know it, I know it, we all know it... how do we fix this?

Well, I began showing them what workflows I'd use. Since the solutions I sold specifically were to assist in conversion and reduce admin work, I made customer profiles that would tell me how they sold, who they sold to, and importantly, what their competitors were doing to solve for what I'm trying to fix (Conversion). Also, what buying signals to be on the lookout for (Basically doing marketing's job, but better). I began targeting companies that were scaling or opening new locations, hiring and posting for middle management, if publicly traded, the performance of the stock, etc. etc.

After isolating TAM, ICP Profiles, I'd start going down the line the org chart to see who'd benefit the most from implementing this and start posting where they see, get them used to seeing me, having perspectives they share, and then finally sending a message talking about how in sync we are and how I got curious and began looking at how his organization has been scaling with all the new hires and ask things relative to what I'm selling such as "Out of sheer professional curiosity, how are you tackling the issue of scaling without losing what made you guys great to begin with? I know how important it is to be consistent and provide the same amazing experience everywhere as a growing business, what guard rails do you have in place for that?"

Bam. Conversation earned.

This was my process, and I drilled that into my reps. I never cared about empty activity metrics, all they signal is noise. How many valuable conversations are happening right now and how is that pipeline being built? I rather hear a rep tell me about how they have the buy-in from a CTO and an Accounting Manager after a week of calculated activity vs I just sent 200 emails, sent out calendar invites, and asked for updates from x people in my pipeline.

Heavy activity metrics are just noise, and lead to customer churn at renewal anyway.
God bless the Customer Success team, they really do a lot of heavy lifting.

What This Taught Me

The best sales conversations rarely feel like sales conversations.

Customers don't enjoy being interrogated, and they certainly don't enjoy being pitched before they feel understood. The organizations that consistently earn trust are the ones that demonstrate understanding before asking for information.

That fundamentally changed how I think about outreach, discovery, and positioning. Today, I spend significantly more time understanding an organization's business before I ever contact them. I want every conversation to begin with relevance rather than curiosity. When customers feel like you've already done the work to understand their world, discovery becomes collaborative instead of transactional, trust develops faster, and conversations naturally become more strategic.

I've been a rep before, from appointment setting in BDC to sales, whatever is rewarded is what's going to get gamified (ever had someone schedule the same appointment every other quarter and add it to pipeline contribution for their show rate bonus?). Activity can mislead you to thinking it's measuring effort (that's definitely possible, but you need to look at all of it). Is the person with 2 hours of talk time and only 15 dials worse than the one with 4 hours of talk time and 80 dials or do they just suck at fast tracking their research phase?

More importantly, emails, texts, calls, voicemails... this information is good to have and all, but what I care more about is "What was the outcome"?

Did we uncover the real buying problem?
Did we establish expectations?
Did the customer understand the value?
Did they leave more confident than when they arrived?

Or are you trying to catch someone's attention who's ghosting you because they gave you a chance and discovered they shouldn't trust you to solve their problem.

Information discovered and Mutual Agreements became the measure of deal velocity, not how many times they've been contacted without anything new happening.

What This Taught Me

People naturally optimize for whatever you choose to measure.

If an organization rewards activity, people will maximize activity. If it rewards meetings, calendars fill up. If it rewards pipeline, pipeline grows—whether those opportunities are real or not.

That experience permanently changed how I think about KPIs. Metrics should never exist simply because they're easy to collect. Every measurement should reinforce the behavior the organization actually wants to create. Today, whenever I design dashboards or reporting systems, I start by asking a different question: "If everyone optimized this metric, would the business actually improve?" If the answer is no, the metric isn't serving the business—it's distracting it.
